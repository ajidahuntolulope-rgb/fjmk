Immunisation schedule

When to come in.

This follows the Nigerian national routine immunisation schedule. We will keep track and remind you, but it helps to know what is coming.

At birth
BCG · Hepatitis B · OPV 0

Tuberculosis, hepatitis B and the first oral polio dose, given before you leave the hospital or at your first visit.

6 weeks
Penta 1 · OPV 1 · Rotavirus 1 · PCV 1

Diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis, hepatitis B and Hib in one injection, plus polio, rotavirus and pneumococcal. Four things, two injections.

10 weeks
Penta 2 · OPV 2 · Rotavirus 2 · PCV 2

The second round. Expect a slightly grizzly evening — that is normal and it passes.

14 weeks
Penta 3 · OPV 3 · PCV 3 · IPV

Completing the primary series. After this, the next routine visit is at nine months.

9 months
Measles 1 · Yellow fever · Meningitis A

Three vaccines at once. Measles in particular is worth protecting against early.

15 months
Measles 2 · Meningitis A booster

The final routine doses. Bring the immunisation card so we can update it.

Routine immunisation is free for eligible children under the national programme. We will tell you honestly what is covered and what is not before anything is given.

Growth monitoring

We plot the line.

Why a chart beats a number.

A single weight tells you almost nothing. A child in the tenth percentile who has always been in the tenth percentile is fine. A child who has dropped from the fiftieth to the tenth over three months needs looking at, even though both numbers are "normal".

That is why we plot it every single visit, against the WHO reference curve, and why we will show you the chart rather than just telling you a weight.

Your first visit

What happens
when you arrive.

1

Weigh and measure

Weight, height and head circumference, plotted straight onto the chart before you see the doctor.

2

You talk, we listen

The doctor asks about feeding, sleep, development and anything worrying you. No question is too small.

3

Examination

A full check, done at your child's pace. If they need a minute to settle, they get a minute.

A written plan

What we found, what to do next, and when to come back. In writing, so you do not have to remember it.

When to bring them in tonight

Trust your instinct first. If you are worried, call us — we would rather see a child who turns out to be fine than miss one who is not.

  • A baby under three months with any fever at all.
  • A child who is unusually sleepy, floppy, or difficult to wake.
  • Fast or laboured breathing, or chest drawing in.
  • Not passing urine, not feeding, or persistent vomiting.
  • A rash that does not fade when you press it.
  • A convulsion, or a fever with a stiff neck.
