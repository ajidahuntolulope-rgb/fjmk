IVF in Nigeria: what to expect

TA
Dr Tolulope Ajidahun
Dr. Tolu is a doctor and reviews this content before it is published.

In vitro fertilisation, or IVF, is one of the most searched fertility treatments in Nigeria. Along with that interest comes a lot of confusion. Some clinics promise miracles. Some people are told it is a last resort, or that it is forbidden. The truth sits somewhere in the middle, and it depends heavily on your particular situation.

IVF is a serious medical treatment. It is not a quick fix, it does not work for everyone, and in Nigeria it is usually paid for entirely out of pocket. Before you commit money and hope, you should understand what the process involves, who it is likely to help, and how to tell a good clinic from a slick one.

This article walks through the stages of IVF, what it feels like, the realistic range of outcomes, and the questions to ask before you start.

What IVF actually is

IVF means fertilising an egg with sperm outside the body, then placing the resulting embryo into the womb. It bypasses problems that stop sperm and egg meeting naturally, which is why it is especially useful when the fallopian tubes are blocked.

There are different versions. In the simplest, eggs and sperm are mixed and left to fertilise on their own. In another, a single sperm is injected directly into an egg, which is used when the sperm count or quality is poor. Which one is used for you depends on your and your partner's test results.

IVF is not the same as, and does not replace, the basic fertility tests. Those still come first, because they tell the doctor whether IVF is even the right option.

When IVF is considered

IVF is usually considered when other approaches have not worked, or when there is a specific problem it can address well. Common reasons include blocked fallopian tubes, severe problems with sperm, ovulation problems that have not responded to simpler treatment, and endometriosis. Sometimes no clear cause is found and treatment is offered after a period of trying naturally.

It is not always the first step, and it should not be. If the problem is simple and can be corrected another way, that is usually cheaper and less invasive. A good doctor will tell you when a simpler route makes sense.

The stages, step by step

The first stage is preparation. You will have tests to confirm the picture, including your partner's semen analysis. The clinic assesses your ovarian reserve, usually with blood tests and a scan, to plan how much stimulation you will need. Cycle timing is planned against your period.

Next comes ovarian stimulation. You take hormone medicines, usually as daily injections, to encourage several eggs to mature instead of the single egg of a normal cycle. This is the part many women find hardest, because of the daily injections and the monitoring appointments. Scans and blood tests are done during this time to track how the follicles are growing and to adjust the dose. Your clinic will teach you how to give the injections.

When the follicles are ready, a trigger injection is given at a precise time to mature the eggs, and the eggs are collected. This is usually done with a needle guided by ultrasound, often with sedation or a light anaesthetic. It is uncomfortable but brief.

The eggs are then fertilised in the laboratory. Some will fertilise and some will not. The embryos are watched as they develop. Not every egg makes a good embryo, and this is one of the most anxious parts of the process.

One or more embryos are then transferred into the womb, usually through the cervix, without surgery. The remaining suitable embryos may be frozen for future use, if that is part of your plan.

Then comes the wait. About two weeks later, a pregnancy test is done. This wait is often the hardest part emotionally.

What it feels like

The injections cause some bruising and bloating. The ovaries become enlarged during stimulation, so you may feel full and tender in the lower belly. After collection, there can be cramping and light spotting. Most women are able to rest for a day and return to normal activity.

The emotional load is heavier than the physical one. There is hope, then uncertainty, then either relief or grief. Many couples find the process strained, and some feel isolated because it is not something they discuss openly. This is not weakness. It is a normal response to a hard treatment.

Success, honestly

There is no single success rate for IVF, and you should be wary of any clinic that quotes one figure for everyone. The chance of a live birth depends on your age, the cause of infertility, the number and quality of embryos available, and the experience of the clinic. Age is the single biggest factor, because egg quality falls over time.

That is why a responsible clinic gives you a personal estimate based on your situation, not a marketing number. It is also why you should ask what happens if the first cycle does not work, and what a second attempt would add.

No honest doctor can promise you a baby from IVF. Anyone who does is selling, not treating.

IVF in the Nigerian setting

In Nigeria, fertility treatment is generally not covered by NHIA or HMO insurance, so most couples pay out of pocket. Costs vary widely between clinics and cities and depend on how much medicine you need, whether freezing is used, and how many cycles you attempt. Because fertility medicines are a large part of the bill, ask for a written breakdown before you start, and ask which medicines are included.

Because people often reach IVF after lengthy trying, there is a market for clinics that overpromise. Protect yourself with a few plain questions: who does the procedure, what is their experience, what is their own result for someone like me, what is the total cost of one cycle including medicines and tests, what happens if it fails, and is there a counsellor. A clinic that welcomes these questions is usually better than one that brushes them off.

There are also religious and cultural considerations. Some couples consult their faith community alongside treatment, and this is their choice. What matters is that traditional or spiritual advice does not replace medical assessment, and that no one is shamed for seeking care.

When to seek urgent care

IVF itself is not an emergency, but complications can occur, and so can warning signs unrelated to treatment. Seek urgent care if you have:

  • Severe abdominal pain, especially with bloating, breathlessness, or vomiting, which can suggest ovarian over-stimulation or another serious problem.
  • Fever with pelvic pain or a foul discharge after procedures.
  • Heavy vaginal bleeding.
  • Sudden severe one-sided pain with fainting, which may suggest an ectopic pregnancy, especially after a positive test but before a scan confirms the pregnancy is in the womb.
  • Difficulty breathing or chest pain.

Do not wait these out. If in doubt, call your clinic or go to a hospital.

Next steps

If you are considering IVF, book a proper fertility assessment for both of you first, and ask for a written cost breakdown and a personal estimate of your chances. Take your partner to the appointment and take a list of questions. If anyone promises a guaranteed pregnancy, get a second opinion. For any of the urgent signs above, seek care immediately.
