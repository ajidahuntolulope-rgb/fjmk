What Pages Should a Clinic Website Have?

A clinic website needs clear service, team, location and contact pages. Build the structure around what visitors need to decide and do next.

FJMK · Healthcare design and digital practice

Short answer: A clinic website should make its services, clinicians, location, hours and next step easy to find. Give each important service its own useful page when it answers a distinct question; do not split one thin description into many near-identical pages.

Most clinic websites are organised around what the organisation wants to say: a welcome, a list of departments, a gallery. A visitor arrives with a smaller job. They need to check whether the clinic offers the service they need, whether it feels credible, and how to make contact.

We use that decision path when we plan healthcare website concepts at FJMK. It is a design test, not a claim that our concepts have produced patient or booking results.

Start with three jobs: recognise, verify, act

  1. Recognise: Can a visitor tell what the clinic does and whether the page is relevant to them?
  2. Verify: Can they check who provides the service, where it is available and what to expect?
  3. Act: Can they call, message or request an appointment without hunting for the route?

If a page does not help with one of those jobs, ask whether it belongs in the main navigation or whether it is internal information that can live elsewhere.

The core pages most clinics need

1. A homepage that routes people

State the clinic’s real name, main services and location. Give visitors clear routes to the services, contact details and appointment process. A homepage should orient people; it cannot explain every specialty well.

2. Service or specialty pages

Use a separate page when a service has its own audience, questions, clinician, location, preparation steps or booking route. Explain what the clinic actually offers, where it is offered, who to contact and what a first enquiry involves. Avoid publishing a page for every keyword variation if the underlying service and answer are the same.

3. Clinician and team information

For each clinician featured, publish only verified names, roles, qualifications and relevant experience. Make it clear who is responsible for care and how the person fits the service. Do not pad biographies with unverified claims.

4. Location and practical visit information

Show the correct address, directions, phone number, opening hours and any practical access details. If the clinic has multiple real branches, give each branch accurate information. Do not create a location page for a place where the clinic does not operate.

5. Contact and appointment information

Say whether a person can call, use WhatsApp, submit a request or book a confirmed time. Explain what happens after someone gets in touch. A request form is not the same thing as a confirmed appointment.

6. Patient information and privacy

Explain relevant processes such as what to bring, how to prepare, payment or insurance information if the clinic chooses to publish it, and how personal information is handled. Keep clinical instructions under an accountable clinical review process.

How to decide whether a service needs its own page

Give it a dedicated page if the answer to at least one of these is yes:

  • Would someone search for this service by name?
  • Does the clinic provide it at only some locations?
  • Does it have a distinct clinician, timetable or booking route?
  • Would a visitor need different preparation or practical information?
  • Can the clinic provide accurate, specific information that is not already on another page?

That last question is the quality check. A page that only swaps “dentist” for “paediatrician” is not a useful service page. It is a duplicate with a different label.

A five-minute mobile test

Open the site on a phone and ask someone unfamiliar with it to find one service, the clinician or team responsible, the clinic’s location, and the correct way to request an appointment. Watch where they hesitate. Those pauses point to missing information or weak page paths more clearly than another round of decorative redesign.

Google recommends crawlable links between important pages and descriptive page titles. That helps discovery, but it does not guarantee indexing or ranking. The page still needs to answer the visitor’s question well.

Further reading: Google’s guide to helping Google understand your site.

What should a clinic fix first?

Start with the page that blocks a real decision. If visitors cannot tell whether you offer a service, fix that service page. If they can find the clinic but cannot work out how to contact it, fix the contact route. More pages are useful only when they add a distinct, accurate answer.
