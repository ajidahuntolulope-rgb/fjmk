PCOS: symptoms, diagnosis and fertility

TA
Dr Tolulope Ajidahun
Dr. Tolu is a doctor and reviews this content before it is published.

Polycystic ovary syndrome, usually shortened to PCOS, is one of the most common hormonal conditions in women of reproductive age. If you have just been told you may have it, or you have spent months wondering why your periods are so unpredictable, you are not imagining it. Something real is happening in your body.

The name is part of the problem. "Polycystic" makes it sound as though your ovaries are full of cysts, and that frightens people. They are not cysts in the dangerous sense. They are small, immature follicles that have paused partway through development because ovulation is not happening on schedule. Many women with the diagnosis do not even have this particular finding on a scan.

What PCOS really describes is a hormone imbalance that plays out in three areas: how often you ovulate, how much androgen (the family of hormones that includes testosterone) your body makes, and how your body handles insulin. You may have all three, or only some. That is why two women with the same diagnosis can look and feel very different.

This article explains what PCOS is, how doctors confirm it, what it means for your periods and fertility, and what can actually be done about it.

What PCOS actually is

Every month, in a normal cycle, a group of follicles in the ovary begins to grow. One of them becomes dominant, releases an egg, and the rest fade away. In PCOS this process stalls. The follicles start to mature but never quite finish, so no egg is released. Because ovulation does not happen, the lining of the womb does not shed on a regular schedule, and your periods become infrequent or unpredictable.

At the same time, the ovaries tend to produce more androgens than usual. This is what drives the acne, the extra hair growth on the face and body, and the hair thinning on the scalp. Insulin resistance sits underneath a lot of this. When the body's cells respond poorly to insulin, the pancreas makes more of it, and high insulin levels push the ovaries to make even more androgen. This is the same reason many women with PCOS find weight difficult to manage.

PCOS is not caused by anything you did. It runs in families, so there is often a mother or sister with similar problems, sometimes diagnosed and sometimes not.

The symptoms, and why they vary

The most common clue is a change in your cycle. Periods may come every few months, or not at all, or they may be heavy and erratic when they do arrive. Some women have regular bleeding but still are not ovulating properly.

Then there are the signs of higher androgens. Acne along the jawline and lower face that carries on past the teenage years. Coarse hair on the chin, upper lip, chest, or lower belly. Thinning hair at the crown. These changes can be upsetting out of proportion to how noticeable they are to anyone else, and that matters.

Weight gain, particularly around the middle, is common but not universal. Some women with PCOS are lean. Insulin resistance can also cause dark, velvety patches of skin in the folds of the neck, armpits, or groin. This is a sign worth mentioning to your doctor.

There is also a link with mood. Anxiety and depression are more common in women with PCOS. That is not simply a reaction to the symptoms. It deserves to be treated in its own right.

How PCOS is diagnosed

PCOS is diagnosed by putting the picture together, not by a single test. The internationally used approach asks for two of three features, once other conditions have been ruled out.

The first is irregular ovulation, judged against how long it has been since your periods began. A cycle that is persistently very long, or fewer than about eight periods in a year, points this way.

The second is clinical or laboratory evidence of high androgens. Clinical means what a doctor can see, such as excess hair growth or acne. Laboratory means a raised testosterone level on a blood test.

The third is the appearance of the ovaries on a scan, or a raised AMH (anti-Mullerian hormone) level. If you have both irregular cycles and clear signs of high androgens, a scan is not always necessary to confirm the diagnosis.

Because the picture is complicated, your doctor will usually also check the thyroid, prolactin, and sometimes other hormones to make sure something else is not imitating PCOS. This is not a waste of time. Treating the wrong thing delays relief.

In teenagers, doctors are more cautious. Ovaries that look "polycystic" are common and normal in the years just after periods start, so the diagnosis is usually made more carefully and might be revisited later.

PCOS and your weight and metabolic health

PCOS is not just about periods. Over the long term, insulin resistance raises the risk of type 2 diabetes and, as women get older, of cholesterol problems and high blood pressure. This is why the diagnosis is worth knowing even if your periods do not bother you much.

Losing a modest amount of weight, if you carry extra weight, can genuinely improve the whole picture. Ovulation often returns, cycles become more regular, and fertility improves. That is worth hearing clearly, because it can feel like a lot of pressure. It is not about reaching an ideal number. A small, sustained change can be enough to shift things.

PCOS, periods and fertility

Here is the most important thing to understand: PCOS is a common cause of difficulty conceiving, but it is not the same as being infertile. Many women with PCOS get pregnant, sometimes on their own, sometimes with help. The problem is usually ovulation, and ovulation is something doctors know how to address.

If your periods are irregular and you want to conceive, the first step is often to confirm whether you are ovulating at all. From there, treatment focuses on triggering ovulation, either through lifestyle measures or with medication prescribed and monitored by a clinician. Those medicines work better when they are properly supervised, so this is not something to arrange on your own.

If you are not trying to conceive, the aim shifts to protecting the womb lining and managing symptoms. Regular shedding of the lining matters because, when periods are very infrequent for a long time, the lining can thicken, and that carries a small long-term risk. So "bringing on a period" now and then is not just cosmetic.

Hormonal treatments, including the combined contraceptive pill, are commonly used to regulate cycles and reduce androgen-related symptoms. These must be chosen and prescribed with your history in mind, especially if you have migraines, high blood pressure, or a clotting risk.

Practical guidance

There is no single fix for PCOS, and anyone promising a permanent cure is not being straight with you. What works is a combination of things matched to your symptoms and your goals.

Movement helps, but it does not have to be punishing. Regular activity improves how your body uses insulin. Food choices that go easy on refined sugar and heavily processed carbohydrates tend to help insulin resistance. These are gradual changes, not a strict regimen you abandon in a month.

If dark skin patches, worsening acne, or new coarse hair appear quickly, say so. Rapidly progressing androgen signs need a proper look to rule out rarer causes. That is not scaremongering, it is good practice.

And if your mood is low, or you are struggling with the way PCOS affects how you see yourself, mention that too. It is part of the condition, and it can be helped.

When to seek urgent care

PCOS itself is not an emergency, but some situations need attention without delay.

  • Very heavy bleeding that soaks through a pad or tampon every hour for several hours, or passes large clots, needs urgent assessment.
  • Severe lower abdominal pain, especially one-sided, or pain with a fever, needs prompt care.
  • If you are pregnant, bleeding, severe headache, blurred vision, or reduced movement of the baby means go to a hospital now.
  • If you have stopped having periods for many months and then start bleeding heavily or continuously, get reviewed.

Next steps

If you recognise your own story here, book an appointment and ask directly whether PCOS is a possibility. Bring a note of when your periods came over the last year, any hair or skin changes, and any family history. That information speeds up everything. You do not have to accept irregular, painful, or unpredictable cycles as simply your lot.
