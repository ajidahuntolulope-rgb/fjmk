Medical Content That Builds Trust Without Spreading Misinformation

OA
Oluwatobi Ajidahun
FJMK · Healthcare marketing

Health content on a clinic website does two jobs at once: it answers a patient's question, and it proves the clinic is a place that knows what it is talking about. Done well, it brings search traffic for years and turns a first-time visitor into a booked patient. Done badly, it damages the same trust it was meant to build — and in healthcare, a mistake can genuinely harm someone.

This article explains how to write medical content that builds trust instead of burning it, following the rules that govern what a clinic may and may not publish.

Why health content is a trust decision, not a traffic play

Most clinics approach health content as a search play. They write an article about a condition because "patients search for it", cram in keywords, produce thin overviews, and move on. The traffic arrives. The content rarely converts, and the credibility damage outlasts the ranking.

Health content is different from every other page on a clinic site. On the services page, a patient is deciding whether to book. On the homepage, they are deciding whether to trust. On a health information page, they are deciding whether to trust the clinic with a health problem at all.

A patient reading "What causes a persistent cough" is looking for reassurance, not a diagnosis. They want to know: is this serious, is it common, what do I do next? If the article answers those questions plainly, it builds trust. If it oversells, understates, or copies a page from another site, it destroys trust — and in healthcare, trust is the exact thing a patient is deciding whether to give.

The three rules for medical content

The Medical Safety Standard sets three rules for health content on this site. They are not optional, and they are not a style preference. They are an operational requirement.

1. Everything is informational, never prescriptive. A health information page may explain how a common condition is typically evaluated, what a patient might expect during an assessment, and what to do if symptoms persist. It may not tell a reader what to do about a specific symptom, may not suggest a diagnosis, may not assert that one treatment is better than another. "If you have these symptoms, see a clinician" is the acceptable frame. Anything else is a medical claim.

2. No miracle-cure or "guaranteed result" language. Claims like "cure", "permanent fix", "guarantees", or "effective against" require sourced regulatory or clinical evidence, and even then they must be phrased around the evidence, never as marketing. The existing pages-every-clinic-website-needs article in this bank states the same boundary: "If you are not sure about a fact, do not publish it. This is the one area where a mistake can genuinely harm someone."

3. Provenance is part of the copy. When a figure, a guideline, or a technique is cited, say where it comes from. "NAFDAC regulates medications in Nigeria" is a factual, contextual statement. "Experts agree" without a named source is not. The patient reading your health content should be able to tell where the information came from, and if they cannot, assume the worst.

How to structure health content for patients

The same structure that makes health content trust-building is the same structure that makes it search-visible. Health content that earns clicks from AI systems and search engines is the content that is easy to extract, easy to verify, and easy to quote.

Lead with the answer, then add detail. The first paragraph should answer the reader's question directly. If the post answers "What causes a persistent cough?" in the first two sentences, the reader stays. If it spends three paragraphs on background, they leave.

Use plain language, and define terms on first use. "Hypertension" is fine on a doctor profile page. On a patient-facing health page, write "high blood pressure", then put "(hypertension)" in brackets. The patient reading this page is not a clinician.

Break information into scannable blocks. Short paragraphs. Subheadings that mirror the questions patients ask. Bullet lists for symptoms, steps, and do-nots. The same structure that makes a page extractable by an AI system also makes it readable on a phone — which is how most patients in Nigeria will read it.

Never present a range as a recommendation. "Most patients are advised to book an appointment within a few days" is a statement about practice. "Book an appointment within a few days" is a recommendation. The first builds trust; the second builds liability.

Include a "why this page exists" note. A short line at the top that says "This page explains how our clinic typically approaches persistent coughs. It is for information, not a substitute for a consultation." This single line resets the reader's expectations and protects both of you.

Common mistakes that spread misinformation

  • Symptom checklists that end in a recommendation. "If you have a fever, take paracetamol" is a treatment suggestion, and it is also a medical claim you cannot make without regulatory evidence. Better: "Fever is a common symptom of many conditions. If it lasts more than a few days, or if you are worried, see a clinician."
  • Copying pages from other sites. Thin, copied, or inaccurate health content damages your credibility faster than having no content at all. The risk is not only legal; it is reputational. A patient who finds the same article on three other clinic sites cannot trust any of them.
  • No dates on health content. Health information ages. A page about a treatment that was standard in 2015 is misleading in 2026. Every health information page should show when it was last reviewed. [pages-every-clinic-website-needs]
  • Patient stories used as marketing without consent. Any testimonial, case study, or patient story must be anonymized or explicitly consented. Never share health details (health conditions, treatments, lab results) without written consent. When in doubt, strip it out. [Medical Safety Standard]
  • Mixing medical content with a services pitch. A page about "What to expect during an antenatal visit" should not have a "Book Now" button in the first paragraph. The reader is not ready to commit, and the page is not patient-ready for the ask.

Trade-offs and edge cases

Breadth versus depth. A single page on "Childhood Illnesses" is easy to write but useless to a parent. Cover the common conditions as separate pages and link between them. But writing ten thin pages is worse than one thorough one.

Up-to-date versus timeless. Some health content needs to be refreshed every few months (treatment standards, guideline changes, new medications). Some is genuinely evergreen (how the body works, common conditions). Track which is which. A "last reviewed" date is the minimum signal; the substance underneath must actually support it. [pages-every-clinic-website-needs]

Local versus general. Health content written for a Lagos clinic should reflect Lagos realities: common conditions in the region, typical presenting symptoms, where patients in your area most often seek care. A page that reads like a US textbook will not build trust with your patients.

Calling in a clinician. This is a marketing writer's article, and it cannot replace a clinician's review. Where medical accuracy is central, the article should state that clinician review is appropriate. A marketing claim about health content is not a clinical claim, but it is a claim about medical information, and the clinic bears the risk either way. [Medical Safety Standard]

Can I write health content about a condition without giving medical advice?

Yes. Explain how a condition is typically understood, what a patient might experience, and what the next step usually is — "If you have these symptoms, see a clinician." That is informational. Telling a reader what to do about their symptoms is not.

How often should health content be updated?

Every health information page should have a visible "last reviewed" date, and pages that cover changing information (treatments, guidelines, new medications) should be refreshed every few months. Pages that cover stable topics can be reviewed annually. The substance underneath must actually change when the date changes.

Do I need consent before publishing patient testimonials?

Yes, explicitly, for anything that identifies a patient or reveals health details. Anonymized, general statements ("patients commonly report being helped by our staff") are acceptable when framed as general experience, not clinical evidence. Anything more specific requires written consent.

Can I recommend one treatment over another?

Only with sourced regulatory or clinical evidence, and even then phrased around the evidence, never as marketing. "Treatment A is suited to X; Treatment B is suited to Y" is the safe frame. "Treatment A is better than Treatment B" is generally unsupported.

Should health content include dates?

Yes. Patients need to know whether the information is current, and so do AI systems. A page that last changed in 2019 reads as a clinic that may have stopped caring.

What to do next

Health content is the one area where a mistake can genuinely harm someone. Start small: pick the three most common patient questions your front desk answers, and write them as trustworthy, plain-language pages. You do not need a library of articles to start; you need three pages that are accurate, current, and clearly informational. The rest can wait.
