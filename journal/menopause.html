Menopause: symptoms and management

TA
Dr Tolulope Ajidahun
Dr. Tolu is a doctor and reviews this content before it is published.

Menopause is not an illness. It is a normal stage of life, the point at which a woman's periods stop for good and her body settles into a new hormonal balance. What makes it worth talking about is that the years around it can be genuinely uncomfortable, and many women suffer in silence because they assume nothing can be done, or because of the way menopause is spoken about in their community.

That silence helps no one. Hot flushes, broken sleep, low mood, and vaginal dryness are common, they are real, and there is a lot that can be done to ease them. This article explains what happens during menopause, which symptoms are typical, and the options for managing them, including the ones that need a doctor's supervision.

What menopause actually is

Menopause is defined by what happens after it: twelve months without a period, with no other cause. The average age falls in the mid-to-late forties to early fifties for most women, though it can occur earlier or later. That is a general pattern, not a rule for any individual.

The years leading up to it are called perimenopause. This is when the ovaries begin winding down and hormone levels swing unpredictably. Perimenopause commonly starts in a woman's forties but can begin earlier. It is during this phase that most bothersome symptoms appear, and it can last years.

Menopause can also be brought on early by surgery to remove the ovaries, by certain medical treatments, or by conditions that affect ovarian function. When this happens, the drop in hormones is often sudden, and symptoms can be more intense.

The symptoms that are common

The well-known symptom is the hot flush, a sudden wave of heat over the face, neck, and chest, often followed by sweating and a chill. Night sweats are the same thing during sleep, and they are a major cause of broken rest.

Sleep disturbance often goes beyond night sweats. Many women find falling asleep harder, and wake more often, which feeds into the next problem.

Mood changes are real and common. Irritability, low mood, and anxiety around this time are partly hormonal and partly a response to poor sleep and the upheaval of midlife. If low mood is persistent or severe, it deserves proper attention rather than being dismissed as "just hormones".

Vaginal and urinary changes often appear later and last longer. The tissue of the vagina and urinary tract thins and becomes drier, which can make sex uncomfortable and increase urinary infections and the need to pass urine urgently. These symptoms do not fade for many women and are among the most treatable.

Other common complaints include joint aches, palpitations, headaches, a change in body shape with weight shifting to the abdomen, forgetfulness, and dry skin or eyes. Some women sail through with hardly a symptom. Others struggle badly. Both are normal, and neither reflects any failing on the woman's part.

What helps

For mild symptoms, practical measures go a long way. Dressing in layers and avoiding known triggers such as spicy food, caffeine, alcohol, and hot rooms helps with flushes. Regular exercise, keeping to a healthy weight, and protecting sleep matter more than most people expect.

For vaginal dryness, lubricants help with sex, and vaginal moisturisers, which are used regularly rather than at the moment, help day to day. These are available without prescription. Do not use scented products or douches in the vagina; they make dryness and irritation worse.

Bone health deserves attention during and after menopause, because the loss of oestrogen speeds bone thinning. Weight-bearing exercise, such as walking, adequate calcium and vitamin D, and staying active all protect bone. Your doctor may recommend a bone density scan if you have particular risk factors.

For troublesome symptoms, hormonal replacement therapy, known as HRT, is an effective option for many women. It is a prescription treatment, and it must be started and monitored by a doctor, because it is not right for every woman and carries its own balance of benefits and risks. The decision is made case by case, weighing your symptoms, your history, and your personal risk factors. There are also non-hormonal prescription medicines that can reduce flushes. None of these should be started on the basis of a friend's experience or something read online.

The cultural layer

In many Nigerian communities, menopause is spoken of in ways that increase distress: as the end of a woman's usefulness, or as something to be endured without complaint, or as a private matter not to be discussed with anyone, least of all a doctor. Some women turn to herbal preparations of uncertain content instead.

It helps to say this plainly. Menopause is a transition, not a decline. It is also a medical stage that responds to treatment, and no woman should have to endure severe symptoms because of what others might say. If your symptoms are affecting your sleep, your work, your mood, or your relationships, that is reason enough to see a doctor.

Frequently asked questions

Will HRT make me put on weight?

Many women gain some weight around the time of menopause regardless of treatment, because metabolism and body composition change with age. HRT is not the main cause. Diet and physical activity have far more influence than hormonal treatment does.

How long does perimenopause last?

It varies widely, from a couple of years to several. For some women it is brief and mild; for others it stretches out and the symptoms shift over time. There is no fixed timetable, and that unpredictability is part of what makes it hard.

Should I take calcium supplements for my bones?

Food sources of calcium are the first choice, and a supplement may be advised for some women. Vitamin D also matters, and deficiency is common. Rather than choosing a supplement yourself, ask your doctor what your intake should be and whether a blood test is warranted.

When to seek urgent care

Most menopause symptoms are not emergencies, but certain bleeding patterns must be checked without delay.

See a doctor promptly if you have any bleeding after menopause has been confirmed, meaning a year or more without periods. Any bleeding at that point, even a small amount or a single episode, needs assessment and usually a scan or biopsy. It is often caused by something harmless, but it must be looked into, because it can be an early sign of a problem in the womb.

Also seek care if you have bleeding between periods during perimenopause that is heavy or repeated, or if you feel unwell with severe pelvic pain or fever.

Go to an emergency department if you have heavy vaginal bleeding that soaks through pads or clothing, chest pain, or sudden severe shortness of breath, since the risk of certain heart and clot problems rises after menopause.

Next steps

If menopause symptoms are affecting your life, make an appointment with a doctor or your hospital's women's health clinic. Come with a note of your symptoms and how they are affecting your sleep, mood, and daily life. Together you can decide which measures fit you, from lifestyle changes to a monitored prescription treatment. You do not have to push through this alone, and you do not have to accept that feeling unwell is simply part of being this age.
