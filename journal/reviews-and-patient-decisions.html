How Online Reviews Affect Patient Decisions (And How to Get More of Them)

OA
Oluwatobi Ajidahun
FJMK · Local search

A patient in Ikeja searches "gynecologist near me" on Google. Five clinics come up. All are open, all are close, all list the same services. She picks the one with 200 reviews at 4.8 stars. She does not call the clinic with 30 reviews at 4.9.

That close call happens thousands of times a week across Lagos. Reviews decide it. They are the trust layer that makes every other channel you spend on convert better, and they are also a ranking signal that decides whether you show up in the map pack at all. Get them right and your schedule fills. Ignore them and the clinic next door takes the call.

Here is what reviews actually do, the math behind them, and how to get more — ethically, without breaking patient privacy or Google's rules.

What reviews actually do

Reviews work on three fronts at once.

Conversion. Patients read reviews before they call. A clinic with a clean website and a strong Google profile wins the close call; the one with no reviews looks like a risk. For a clinic, a review is a patient telling a stranger "you can trust this place." That is more persuasive than anything you can write about yourself.

Ranking. Google weighs reviews when it decides who appears in the local map pack. More reviews, recent reviews, and a higher rating all push you up. The clinic with the better review profile ranks higher and gets the clicks.

AI visibility. 45% of consumers now use ChatGPT or similar tools to ask for local business recommendations. Those AI answers draw on your reviews and third-party mentions. If your clinic has a thin review profile, AI tools have little to say about you — and they will recommend a competitor instead.

Recency matters more than people think. 73% of consumers only trust reviews from the last month. A clinic with 200 reviews and none since last spring looks inactive. A clinic with 80 reviews and three from this week looks alive. Fresh reviews signal that you are open, serving patients, and worth a call.

The review math

Volume, recency, and rating all count. But they do not count equally.

A clinic with 200 reviews at 4.8 stars out-converts one with 30 reviews at 4.9. Here is why: a single bad review drags a 30-review clinic's average down hard, and patients can see the sample is small. Two hundred reviews at 4.8 shows a track record. Patients trust a proven pattern over a near-perfect score from a handful of people.

The target is a steady drip, not a one-time push. An active clinic should aim for 5 to 10 new reviews a month. That keeps your profile recent, feeds the ranking signal, and gives AI tools fresh material to draw on. A burst of 40 reviews in one week followed by silence does less than ten a month, every month.

How to get more reviews, ethically

The method is simple and it works: ask every patient, every time, at the right moment.

Ask at the right time. The best moment is right after a good visit — at checkout, or within a few hours while the experience is still fresh. Do not ask a patient who just had a difficult appointment. Ask the ones who are happy.

Send the link by text or WhatsApp. Within a few hours of the visit, send a short message with a direct link to your Google review form. One tap and they are on the page. The fewer steps, the more people finish. Text and WhatsApp are the channels your patients already use.

Use the survey-gate method. Send a short post-visit survey: a 0 to 10 score plus a free-text box. Whoever scores 9 or 10 gets the review link. Lower scores get a "tell us more" follow-up and a chance for you to fix the problem before it becomes a public bad review. This is not gating — you are not hiding real feedback. You are catching problems early and routing happy patients to the review form.

Make it one tap. Do not make patients create an account or log in. A direct link, a star rating, a short comment. Remove friction and completion rates climb.

Train the front desk. Your receptionist sees every patient. A one-line ask at checkout — "if you were happy today, a quick Google review would help us a lot" — costs nothing and produces steady results. Make it part of the checkout routine, not an afterthought.

How to respond

Reply to every review, positive and negative, within a day or two. Silence reads as "we do not care."

Positive reviews. Thank the patient and be specific. "Thank you, Mrs. Okafor — glad the physio sessions are helping." Specific beats generic. It shows real people read these.

Negative reviews. Apologize without admitting clinical fault. "We are sorry your visit did not meet expectations" is safe. "We misdiagnosed your condition" is not — that is an admission that can cause serious problems. Take the conversation offline: "Please call us on this number so we can look into it." Then actually fix the issue. A clinic that responds calmly and solves problems turns a bad review into proof of good service.

Never disclose health details. This is non-negotiable in healthcare. Do not repeat a patient's condition, treatment, or even confirm that someone is a patient. "We are sorry you had a frustrating experience — please contact us directly" works without revealing anything.

Privacy in healthcare

Healthcare reviews sit in a special category. A review is not a normal business review.

Never confirm in a reply that someone is a patient. Even that can be a privacy breach. If a reviewer names a condition or treatment, do not repeat it. Reply generally and take specifics offline.

If you want to use a patient's story as a testimonial on your website, get written consent first — especially if it includes anything identifiable. In Nigeria, the NDPR governs how you handle personal data. Treat patient information carefully. A review that reveals health details is a problem for the patient and a risk for you.

What NOT to do

Do not buy reviews. Google detects fake review patterns — bursts from new accounts, repeated phrasing, suspicious timing. A single penalty can drop your clinic out of the local pack for months. The cost of a penalty far outweighs any short-term boost.

Do not incentivize with cash. Offering money or discounts for reviews violates Google's rules and attracts exactly the kind of fake activity that triggers penalties. Ask, do not pay.

Do not hide all negative reviews. Gating that filters out every complaint looks dishonest and gets flagged. The survey-gate method is different — it routes happy patients to the form and gives unhappy ones a fix, but it does not scrub real feedback.

Do not create fake accounts. A handful of fake positive reviews from throwaway accounts is the fastest way to a penalty. Real patients, real reviews, steady pace.

What reviews don't fix

Reviews feed the funnel. They do not close it.

A clinic with a bad front-desk experience gets bad reviews no matter how hard it pushes the review link. A clinic with a slow, confusing, mobile-broken website gets the calls from its good reviews and loses them on the booking page. The review gets the patient to the door; the website and the experience get them through it.

Fix the experience, then ask for reviews. Fix the website, then send patients to it. Reviews and a working web presence work together — one without the other leaks patients.

How many reviews do I need?

Aim for a steady 5 to 10 new reviews a month for an active clinic. Volume builds trust, recency keeps you visible, and a consistent drip beats one big push.

Can I ask patients for reviews?

Yes. Ask happy patients at the right moment — after a good visit, at checkout, or by text or WhatsApp within a few hours. Asking is normal and expected. Buying reviews is not.

What do I do about a bad review?

Reply within a day or two. Apologize without admitting clinical fault, take the conversation offline, and fix the issue. A calm, helpful response turns a bad review into proof of good service.

Is it legal to offer a discount for a review?

No. Offering money or discounts for reviews violates Google's rules and risks a penalty. Ask for reviews honestly; do not pay for them.

Do reviews affect Google rankings?

Yes. Google weighs reviews for local map-pack placement. Volume, recency, and rating all matter. More recent reviews push you up.

How do I get reviews without violating patient privacy?

Never confirm someone is a patient, never repeat health details, and take specifics offline. Get written consent before using any identifiable patient story as a testimonial. Handle all patient data under NDPR rules.

What to do next

Reviews are the trust layer that makes your other marketing convert. Start by fixing the experience patients actually get, then build a steady review routine — ask every patient, send the link by WhatsApp within hours, and reply to everything.

And make sure the website those reviews send people to actually works. A fast, mobile-first site that turns visitors into booked appointments is what turns a full review profile into a full schedule.
