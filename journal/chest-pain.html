Chest pain: causes and when to seek urgent care

TA
Dr Tolulope Ajidahun
Dr. Tolu is a doctor and reviews this content before it is published.

Chest pain is frightening, and it should be treated with respect. But it is worth knowing that not every pain in the chest is a heart attack. The chest holds the heart, the lungs, the food pipe, the ribs and the muscles between them, plus nerves that can carry pain from the stomach or the spine. Pain felt in the same area can come from any of these.

The single most important thing this article will tell you is this: chest pain that could be coming from the heart must be treated as an emergency until it is proven otherwise. It is not something to "wait and see". If you are reading this because you or someone near you has chest pain right now, stop here and get help.

For everyone else, we will look at the warning signs, the other common causes, and how a doctor sorts one from another.

When chest pain means call for help now

Do not wait. Call emergency services or get to the nearest emergency department immediately if chest pain comes with any of these:

  • Pressure, tightness, squeezing or heaviness in the centre of the chest, especially lasting more than a few minutes, or coming and going.
  • Pain spreading to the arm, shoulder, neck, jaw, or upper back.
  • Sweating, nausea, or a sense of dread.
  • Breathlessness.
  • Fainting or near-fainting.
  • A racing or irregular heartbeat.

These are the classic features of a heart attack, known medically as a myocardial infarction. The pain is often described as a weight on the chest rather than a sharp jab, and it may come with a feeling that something is badly wrong. In some people, particularly women, older adults, and people with diabetes, the pain can be mild or absent, and the main signs are breathlessness, tiredness, nausea, or discomfort in the jaw or back. Do not wait for the "classic" picture before acting.

With the heart, time is muscle. The sooner someone is assessed and treated, the more heart muscle is saved.

What else causes chest pain

Once the heart is set aside, a wide range of other things can cause chest pain, and many are not dangerous.

Muscle and rib pain, sometimes called chest wall pain, is common. It is often sharp, worse when you move, twist, press the spot, or take a deep breath, and it may ease when you rest. A recent cough or heavy lifting often comes before it.

Acid reflux and indigestion cause a burning pain behind the breastbone, often after meals or when lying flat, sometimes with a sour taste in the mouth. It can be very convincing and can overlap closely with heart pain, which is exactly why it should not be diagnosed by the patient alone.

Anxiety and panic can produce real chest tightness, often with a rapid heartbeat, fast breathing, tingling in the hands, and a sense of dread. The distress is genuine, but anxiety is a diagnosis of exclusion. Do not assume it until the heart has been checked.

Lung problems such as pneumonia, a collapsed lung, or pleurisy cause pain that is worse on deep breathing and often comes with cough, fever, or breathlessness. A blood clot in the lung, called a pulmonary embolism, is a serious cause and typically brings sudden breathlessness with pain on breathing.

Shingles can cause a band of burning pain on one side of the chest or back, even before any rash appears.

Who is at higher risk

Some people should take chest pain even more seriously. If you have diabetes, high blood pressure, high cholesterol, or a history of smoking; if a close relative had a heart attack or died suddenly at an early age; or if you already have known heart disease or have had a stroke, your threshold for getting checked should be lower, not higher.

In pregnancy, chest pain also needs prompt assessment because of the added strain on the heart and circulation. And in sickle cell disease, chest pain can signal a serious complication called acute chest syndrome, which needs urgent care rather than a wait-and-see approach.

How a doctor sorts it out

Because the causes overlap, doctors rely on the story plus tests. An ECG looks at the heart's electrical activity. Blood tests can show heart muscle damage. A chest X-ray looks at the lungs and the shape of the chest. Depending on the picture, more tests may follow.

You can help by being precise: where the pain is, what it feels like, what brings it on, what relieves it, how long it lasts, and whether it spreads. That description often matters as much as any single test.

What to do right now

If the pain might be coming from the heart, do not drive yourself. Call for help or have someone take you. Chew an aspirin only if a clinician or emergency dispatcher has told you to, and only if you are not allergic to it and have not been advised against it.

If the pain is clearly muscular and mild, rest and simple paracetamol may help. But if you are not sure, treat it as cardiac until someone qualified has checked you. Being wrong in that direction is safe. Being wrong the other way is not.

When to seek urgent care

Treat any of the following as an emergency:

  • Central chest pressure or heaviness lasting more than a few minutes.
  • Chest pain with sweating, breathlessness, nausea, or fainting.
  • Pain spreading to the arm, jaw, neck, or back.
  • Sudden severe breathlessness.
  • Chest pain with a fast or irregular heartbeat.
  • Chest pain after a blow to the chest, or with coughing up blood.

If in doubt, seek care. Nobody has ever been criticised for getting chest pain checked.

Frequently asked questions

Can acid reflux feel exactly like a heart attack?

Yes, and that is why reflux should not be self-diagnosed when chest pain is new or severe. A doctor can tell the difference with a proper assessment.

I am young and fit. Can it still be my heart?

It is less likely, but not impossible. Other causes are more common in younger people, yet the warning signs still apply to everyone.

Should I take aspirin at home?

Only if a clinician or emergency service has told you to, and only if you have no allergy or bleeding problem. Do not self-prescribe it as a routine.

Does the pain need to be severe to be serious?

No. Some heart attacks cause mild or unusual pain, especially in women, older people, and people with diabetes.

Next steps

If you have chest pain that is new, severe, or comes with any red flag, get emergency help now. If you have recurring chest discomfort that has been checked before but is changing, or if you carry risk factors like diabetes, high blood pressure or a family history of heart disease, book an appointment for a proper cardiac assessment rather than waiting for the next episode.
