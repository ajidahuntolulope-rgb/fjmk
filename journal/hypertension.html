Hypertension (high blood pressure): causes and control

TA
Dr Tolulope Ajidahun
Dr. Tolu is a doctor who reviews this content before it is published.

Most people who have high blood pressure do not feel it. There is no headache that tells you for certain, no ache in the neck that warns you. That is the whole problem. You can carry a pressure high enough to damage your heart, your brain and your kidneys for years and feel completely well the entire time. This is why high blood pressure is sometimes called a silent condition, and why it is worth knowing your numbers even when nothing is wrong.

If you have just been told your blood pressure is high, you are probably wondering two things. Do I really need medicine for the rest of my life? And what did I do to bring this on? This article answers both, along with what the two numbers on the cuff actually mean and what you can change yourself.

What blood pressure actually measures

Every time your heart beats, it pushes blood into your arteries. The pressure of that blood against the artery walls is your blood pressure. The top number, the systolic pressure, is the pressure at the moment the heart squeezes. The bottom number, the diastolic pressure, is the pressure when the heart relaxes between beats.

Both numbers matter. A reading is written as systolic over diastolic, for example 150/95. In adults, doctors generally treat a pressure that stays at or above 140/90 as high blood pressure. Readings between roughly 130/85 and 140/90 are often called elevated or borderline, a grey zone that deserves attention but not panic.

One reading is not a diagnosis. Blood pressure moves around during the day. It rises with coffee, pain, stress, a full bladder, or simply being in a hospital. That is why a proper diagnosis is based on repeated readings, taken on separate days, with you sitting quietly for a few minutes first. If your clinic readings are high but your readings at home are normal, your doctor may suspect what is called white coat hypertension, where the setting itself pushes the number up.

The two main types

In the large majority of cases there is no single cause that can be pointed to. This is called primary or essential hypertension, and it develops gradually over years, shaped by age, family history, weight, salt intake, alcohol, inactivity and stress. When someone says the cause is unknown, they do not mean it comes from nowhere. They mean no one factor is solely to blame.

In a smaller group of people, high blood pressure is the result of another condition. Kidney disease is a common one. So are certain hormonal problems such as an overactive thyroid or adrenal gland issues, narrowing of the artery to a kidney, and some medicines including the contraceptive pill and certain anti-inflammatory painkillers. This is called secondary hypertension, and it is more likely to be suspected when the pressure is very high, comes on suddenly, appears at a young age, or does not respond to the usual treatment. Finding and treating the underlying condition can improve the pressure a great deal.

Pregnancy deserves a separate mention. High blood pressure in pregnancy needs prompt medical attention, because conditions such as pre-eclampsia can become dangerous for both mother and baby. Do not wait to report a high reading, a severe headache, blurred vision, or swelling of the face and hands if you are pregnant.

Why it matters even when you feel fine

Sustained high pressure presses against the walls of your arteries all day and all night. Over time this stiffens and narrows them. The damage shows up quietly in a few places: the heart, which grows thicker and weaker under the strain, the brain and the arteries feeding it, and the kidneys, which filter blood through very fine vessels.

This is why untreated high blood pressure raises the risk of heart attack, heart failure, stroke and kidney failure. The reassuring side of this is that treatment works. Lowering blood pressure meaningfully reduces the risk of these events. The point of treating someone who feels well is to prevent something that has not happened yet.

How it is investigated

Once high blood pressure is confirmed, your doctor will usually look for clues about how long it has been running and whether it has already affected the organs that suffer first. Expect a careful examination, urine and blood tests to check the kidneys, blood sugar and cholesterol, and often an electrocardiogram to look at the heart. Some people need extra tests such as an ultrasound of the heart or kidneys if something on examination is abnormal.

These tests are not because the diagnosis is doubtful. They set the baseline. They tell your doctor how your body is handling the pressure and which medicines will suit you best.

What you can change yourself

Lifestyle changes are not a soft alternative to medicine. They are part of the treatment for almost everyone, and for some people with mild elevations they are enough.

Cutting salt is the single most useful change. Most of it is not what you shake on the food but what is already in it: seasoning cubes, stock, smoked and canned foods, processed meats, bread and snacks eaten outside the home. Tasting your food before adding salt and reducing how much you cook with is a real start.

Weight matters. Losing even a modest amount of weight often lowers blood pressure measurably. Regular physical activity helps, most days of the week where you can manage it. Alcohol, if you drink, should be kept low. Smoking does not raise blood pressure much on a single reading but it is a powerful multiplier of heart and stroke risk alongside it, so it deserves separate attention. Stress and poor sleep also keep pressure high, and are worth taking seriously rather than dismissing.

Medicines, and the fear of taking them long term

Many people resist tablets because they fear becoming dependent, or they feel fine and see no reason to continue. The medicine is not a cure that can be stopped once you feel better. It is doing its job precisely when you feel nothing.

Blood pressure medicines work in different ways: some relax the blood vessels, some reduce the workload on the heart, some remove excess fluid and salt. Most people need more than one, and finding the right combination can take a few visits. Your doctor will choose based on your age, your kidney function, whether you have diabetes, and other conditions. Never start, stop or change a prescribed medicine on your own, and never take a friend's tablets because they share your reading. Tell your doctor if a medicine causes dizziness, a persistent cough or swollen ankles; there is usually a different one that suits you better.

Home monitoring, using a proper upper-arm machine and a cuff that fits, helps you and your doctor see the real pattern rather than a single clinic number.

When to seek urgent care

High blood pressure itself is rarely an emergency, but a pressure that is extremely high together with certain symptoms is. Go to a hospital immediately, or call for help, if you have:

  • A very high reading together with chest pain, breathlessness, or pain spreading to the arm or jaw.
  • Sudden weakness or drooping on one side of the face or body, or sudden difficulty speaking or understanding speech. This may be a stroke.
  • A sudden severe headache, confusion, or a seizure.
  • Sudden blurred vision or loss of vision.
  • Severe shortness of breath, or coughing up blood.
  • If you are pregnant: a severe headache, blurred vision, upper abdominal pain, swelling of the face and hands, or reduced baby movements.

Do not try to bring a very high pressure down yourself with extra doses of medicine. That can be dangerous. Get assessed.

Frequently asked questions

Does high blood pressure cause headaches? Headaches can happen with very high pressure, but most people with ordinary high blood pressure have no headache at all. Do not use a headache as your warning system. Use a cuff.

Can I stop my tablets once my pressure is normal? No, not without your doctor's advice. The reading is normal because the medicine is working. Stopping is usually followed by the pressure climbing back.

Is it only a problem for older people? It becomes more common with age, but younger adults get it too, sometimes because of kidney disease, hormones or weight. Age is a risk factor, not a rule.

Next steps

If you have not had your blood pressure checked in the last year, have it checked. It takes a minute and it is painless. If you already have high blood pressure, keep your follow-up appointments even when you feel well, and bring your home readings with you. Bring your medicines too, or a list of them. If you have never been treated and your reading is persistently high, book a proper assessment so the cause, the risks and the right treatment can be worked out for you rather than guessed at.
