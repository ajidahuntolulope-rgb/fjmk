What Pages Does Every Clinic Website Need?

OA
Oluwatobi Ajidahun
FJMK · Healthcare marketing

A clinic website is not a brochure. It is a set of answers to the questions patients ask before they book. A patient lands on your site, checks who you are, whether you treat their problem, whether you take their HMO, and how to reach you. Answer those questions clearly and they book. Leave them unanswered and they call the clinic down the road.

Most clinic owners in Lagos and across Nigeria treat a website as a digital business card. That is a mistake. A business card gets you a phone call. A well-built website gets you a booked appointment. The difference comes down to the pages you build and what each one does.

Here is the structure that works.

The homepage: the eight-second trust test

The homepage has about eight seconds to convince a visitor they are in the right place. In that time they need to know four things: who you are, what you treat, how to book, and proof that you are real.

State your clinic's name and what you specialise in in the first sentence. Show your services in plain words — "malaria treatment," "antenatal care," "dental check-up" — not medical jargon. Put a clear "Book an appointment" button where they cannot miss it. And show real proof: actual photos of your facility, your doctors, your reception. Generic stock photos read as risky to a patient deciding whether to trust you with their health.

The common mistake is a homepage written for the hospital board instead of the patient. Long mission statements, administrator-speak, and a photo of the building. Patients do not care about your mission statement. They care about whether you can help them today.

Services pages: one per major service

Patients search for the service, not your clinic name. Someone with a fever types "malaria treatment near me" into Google. Someone pregnant searches "antenatal care in Ikeja." If your website has one giant page listing every service, that patient never finds you.

Build a separate page for each major service. Each page should say what the service is, what it involves, how long a visit takes, what it costs where you can say so, and how to book it. This is how patients find you in search, and it is how you convert the ones who arrive.

The mistake is treating services as a single list. Break them out. Every major service you offer should be able to answer a patient's question on its own page.

Doctor and consultant profiles

Patients choose the doctor, not the building. Before they book, they want to know who will treat them. Is this person qualified? How long have they been practising? When can I see them?

Give every doctor their own profile: full name, qualifications, years of experience, areas of interest, a real photo, and their consultation hours. This is one of the strongest trust signals a clinic site can carry. A patient who can see and read about a doctor is far more likely to book that doctor.

The mistake is an outdated doctor list — names of consultants who left two years ago, or no profiles at all. If a patient cannot tell who works at your clinic, they assume the worst and move on.

The booking page

Around two-thirds of patients prefer to book online. If your only option is "call us," you are losing the patients who would rather pick a slot themselves.

The booking page should be short. Name, phone number, service, preferred date — five fields, no more. An immediate confirmation message. And a WhatsApp option, because in Nigeria that is often how patients actually want to reach you.

The mistake is a twelve-field form that asks for a full medical history before the patient has even committed. Every extra field costs you bookings. And "we'll get back to you soon" is a dead end — patients who cannot confirm their slot immediately go somewhere that lets them.

Contact and location page

This page answers a simple question: how do I get to you, and can I reach you when I need to?

Include your full address, directions, parking information, a phone number that actually gets answered, and a map. In Nigeria, put your emergency number on this page — and on every other page too. Emergency contact visible everywhere is a habit patients expect from a clinic they can trust.

The mistake is a contact page with a form but no phone number, or a listed number that rings forever. If a patient cannot reach you, they assume you are not there.

The insurance and HMO page

This question ends most searches: "Do you take my HMO?" For many Nigerian patients, whether you are on their HMO panel is the deciding factor between you and the clinic two streets away.

Publish a clear page listing the HMOs and insurance providers you work with, and what each covers. If you are on NHIS panels or specific HMO networks, say so plainly. This page saves your front desk from answering the same question a hundred times a day, and it keeps patients on your site instead of leaving to check elsewhere.

The mistake is hiding this information or leaving it vague. If a patient cannot confirm you take their HMO, they assume you do not.

Health information content

Patients arrive with questions about their symptoms and diagnoses. A page that answers those questions builds trust and feeds your search visibility — a well-written piece on a common condition can bring steady traffic for years.

Write carefully. Health content must be accurate, clearly informational, and never a substitute for a consultation. No misinformation, no miracle cures, no claims you cannot back. If you are not sure about a fact, do not publish it. This is the one area where a mistake can genuinely harm someone.

The mistake is writing for search engines instead of patients, or copying content from other sites. Thin, copied, or inaccurate health content damages your credibility faster than having no content at all.

Reviews and testimonials

Patients trust other patients. Real reviews — with consent, recent, and specific — are among the strongest conversion tools a clinic site has.

Ask satisfied patients if you can share their words. Use their real names where they allow it, and keep the reviews current. A review from 2019 next to nothing from this year reads as a clinic that stopped caring.

The mistake is fabricating testimonials or using stock photos of "patients." Patients can tell, and a fake review is the fastest way to lose the trust you spent the rest of the site building.

FAQs

Your front desk answers the same questions every day. Put those answers on a page. What are your opening hours? Do I need an appointment? Do you take walk-ins? What should I bring? How much does a consultation cost?

Each question answered once, clearly, saves a phone call. And patients who find their answer on your site are more confident about booking.

The mistake is treating FAQs as an afterthought or leaving them generic. Use the real questions your staff hear, in the words patients actually use.

What not to include

  • Auto-playing music. Nobody wants it, and it makes a clinic site feel like a 2008 blog.
  • Twelve-field forms. Friction loses bookings.
  • Navigation that mirrors your hospital's org chart. Patients do not think in departments; they think in problems.
  • Stock-photo-only design. Real photos build trust; generic ones erode it.
  • Outdated doctor lists. Wrong information is worse than none.
  • Hidden prices. Where you can state a price, state it. Patients who cannot find a price often leave.

How many pages does a clinic website need? At minimum, the pages above: homepage, services, doctors, booking, contact, HMO, and FAQs. Most effective clinic sites run between eight and fifteen pages. Quality matters more than count.

Do I need a separate page for each doctor? If you have more than two doctors, yes. Patients choose their doctor, and a profile page is the strongest trust signal you can offer.

Should I put prices on my website? Where it is practical, yes. Published prices reduce friction and filter for serious patients. Where pricing varies, state a range or a starting figure.

Do I need online booking, or is a phone number enough? Around two-thirds of patients prefer to book online. A phone number alone loses the ones who want to pick a slot themselves. Offer both, and add WhatsApp.

What about a blog? A blog is useful for health information content that builds trust and brings search traffic. Only start one if you can keep it accurate and current.

How long does it take to build a clinic website? A focused clinic site with the pages above typically takes four to eight weeks from content to launch. The content — your services, doctors, HMO list — is usually the slowest part, so start gathering it early.

What to do next

You do not need a hundred pages. You need the right pages, each answering one patient question clearly. Get that structure right and your website stops being a brochure and starts filling your schedule.
