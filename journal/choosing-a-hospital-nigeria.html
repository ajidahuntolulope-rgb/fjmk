How to choose a private hospital in Nigeria

TA
Dr Tolulope Ajidahun
Dr. Tolu is a doctor and reviews this content before it is published.

When someone in the family falls ill, the first real decision is often not which drug to buy. It is where to take them. In most Nigerian cities there is no shortage of private hospitals, clinics and consulting rooms within a short drive. The hard part is telling which of them can actually look after you, and which will send you somewhere else the moment things turn serious.

This is not a ranking. Nobody can tell you which hospital is best for your family, because that depends on where you live, what you can afford and what your HMO covers. What this article can do is give you a short list of questions to ask while everyone is well. Choosing calmly in advance is far easier than choosing in a panic at midnight.

The worst time to judge a hospital is during an emergency. So the aim here is to do the thinking now, before you need it.

Start with official registration

Every legitimate hospital or clinic in Nigeria is registered with the health regulator in its state, and regulated further depending on what services it offers. In Lagos, that state regulator is HEFAMAA. In the Federal Capital Territory, the health secretariat plays a similar role. Other states have their own bodies.

A registered facility should be able to show you its certificate of registration or display it in the reception area. There is nothing rude about asking. A hospital that cannot show proof that it is licensed to operate is a risk you do not need to take.

Beyond the state licence, look for accreditation that matches the services offered. Laboratories should meet the standards of the Medical Laboratory Science Council of Nigeria. Pharmacies should be registered with the Pharmacists Council of Nigeria. Radiology departments that use X-ray machines and other imaging equipment need radiation-safety approval from the Nigerian Nuclear Regulatory Authority. A facility that does a bit of everything without meeting the corresponding standards is worth questioning.

Check whether your HMO or NHIA plan actually covers it

Being NHIA-accredited overall is not the same as being covered under your particular plan. Your HMO has its own network of hospitals, and taking treatment outside that network usually means paying out of your own pocket even though you have insurance.

So the practical step is simple. Before you need care, call your HMO or check your provider list and confirm which hospitals near you are on your plan. Save two or three of them in your phone, in case one is full or far from where you are when something happens.

Ask three questions when you call. Is this hospital in my network? Is it a primary provider or can I walk in directly? And is there a co-payment I should expect? The person who answers the phone may not know all the details, so if the answers are vague, ask for it in writing or check the HMO's own list.

If you are covered through a state social health insurance scheme rather than a private HMO, the same principle applies. Confirm the accredited facilities on your scheme, because those lists change and a hospital that used to be on the list may have dropped off.

Find out who will actually see you

A hospital is only as good as the people on duty when you arrive. It is worth knowing whether there is a resident doctor on site at night, or whether the doctor is called in from home. For a clinic that handles only minor cases, calling someone in can be acceptable. For a hospital that advertises itself as handling emergencies, there should be clinical staff present around the clock.

Ask who the medical director is and what the staffing looks like. A busy outpatient practice may have a general practitioner in the day and a nurse on duty at night. That is fine for many problems, as long as the hospital is honest about what it can and cannot handle, and refers you onward quickly when the problem is beyond it.

Continuity matters too. If you have a chronic condition such as diabetes, hypertension or sickle cell disease, you want one place that keeps your records and knows your history. Flitting between five different clinics every time you feel unwell means nobody sees the whole picture.

Can the hospital handle an emergency?

This is the question most people never ask until it is too late. A good private hospital should have a functioning emergency area, oxygen, resuscitation equipment and a way to get you to a higher level of care if it cannot manage you.

Walk through what you would want to know. Is there oxygen? Is there a working theatre, or an arrangement with another hospital that has one? If you arrived with chest pain or difficulty breathing at 2am, what would happen?

You do not need to be a clinician to ask these things. You are simply checking that the hospital has thought about the same scenarios you are worried about. A facility that dismisses the question or becomes defensive is telling you something.

Laboratory, pharmacy and diagnostics under one roof

For many everyday illnesses, it helps when the hospital can do the basic tests it needs without sending you across town. A functioning laboratory, a stocked pharmacy and simple imaging such as X-ray and ultrasound make care faster and more joined-up.

That said, a small clinic without a laboratory can still be a good first stop if it knows when to refer. The danger sign is a facility that does not have the test, does not have the pharmacy, and still tries to manage a serious illness on guesswork rather than sending you to where the answer is available.

Ask what it will cost before treatment starts

Private care in Nigeria is largely paid out of pocket unless you have cover, and costs vary widely between facilities and states. It is reasonable, and increasingly common, to ask for an estimate before a procedure or admission begins.

Ask how much a consultation costs, what a night on admission costs, and whether the estimate includes drugs, tests and theatre fees or only the bed. Ask whether the hospital will tell you when charges are running higher than expected. A facility that is upfront about money is usually also upfront about everything else.

Keep your receipts, and keep copies of your test results. They belong to your care, and you will want them if you move to a new hospital.

Look at location, access and how you are treated

A hospital forty minutes away may offer better care than one five minutes from you, but only if you can actually get there. For a heart attack or a stroke, minutes matter. Know which facility is closest and can stabilise you.

Also watch how the place treats people. Are patients attended to with respect? Is there somewhere clean to sit? Does the reception staff explain what you are waiting for? Are records kept properly, or does every visit start with the same blank sheet? These ordinary things often predict how safe the place is when the stakes rise.

Warning signs about a facility

Be cautious with any hospital that promises a cure for everything, refuses to give you your results, discourages a second opinion, or presses you towards expensive treatment you did not ask about. Be careful with facilities that cannot show a licence, that keep no records, or that will not refer you upward when your condition needs a specialist. These patterns matter more than any signboard.

When to seek urgent care

Do not spend time comparing hospitals if any of these are happening. Get to the nearest functioning emergency department, or call for help, immediately.

  • Chest pain with sweating, breathlessness, or pain spreading to the arm or jaw
  • Sudden face droop, weakness in one arm or leg, or difficulty speaking
  • Severe abdominal pain with a hard, board-like belly
  • Fever with a stiff neck, confusion, or a rash that does not fade when pressed
  • Heavy bleeding that soaks pads or clothing, or blood in vomit or stool
  • In pregnancy: bleeding, severe headache, blurred vision, reduced baby movement, or convulsions
  • In a child: fever with a rash that does not fade, unusual drowsiness, refusal to feed, fast breathing, or repeated convulsions

If in doubt, go. When you choose well in advance, that decision is already made.

Next steps

If you do not already know which hospital near you would take you in an emergency, take twenty minutes this week to find out. Confirm two hospitals that are on your HMO or NHIA network, check that each is registered and has clinical staff overnight, and save their numbers in your phone. If you are unsure whether a symptom needs urgent attention, speak to a doctor rather than waiting to see how it goes.
